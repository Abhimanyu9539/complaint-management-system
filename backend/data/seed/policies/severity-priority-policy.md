# Severity & Prioritisation Policy

This policy is company-wide and defines the four severity levels, who sets them, and what they change. Severity is the single input that decides which of two waiting customers is served first, so it has to mean the same thing in every department.

## 1. The Four Levels

**Critical** — someone could be harmed, or the company faces regulatory or legal exposure. An active safety symptom, a reported injury, a legal notice or consumer-commission filing, a confirmed batch defect in circulation, an account takeover in progress, or a systemic failure affecting many customers at once.

**High** — the customer cannot use what they paid for, or money is wrong and they are out of pocket, or a deadline they told us about is at risk. A dead unit, money debited with no order confirmed, a subscription still billing after cancellation, an order that never arrived past its window, a third contact on one unresolved issue, or a partner or marketplace-channel claim.

**Normal** — something is wrong and it is affecting the customer, but they are not blocked and nothing is escalating. A functional fault with a workaround, a return request, a delivery inside the tolerance in shipping §1, a coupon or pricing adjustment, a parts order gone wrong.

**Low** — the customer wants something changed, corrected or explained, and waiting costs them little. A cosmetic complaint, a listing correction request, an invoice correction, a question about a policy or a process.

## 2. Critical Is Reserved

Critical means the queue reorders around it and people are pulled off other work, which only works if it stays rare. It is not for a customer who is very angry, a large account, a repeatedly escalated ticket, or a complaint that mentions a lawyer without one being involved. Frustration raises severity to high at most; what makes something critical is the potential for harm or exposure, not the volume of the complaint. Every critical ticket has a named owner from the moment it is set — an unowned critical is indistinguishable from a normal one within an hour.

## 3. Who Sets It

Customers may tell us how urgent their situation is and are believed: a stated deadline, a medical dependence, a business impact all feed the assessment, and no proof is required for any of them (vulnerable §5). But **customers select up to high; critical is only ever assessed internally.** This is not distrust — it is that critical triggers a company response that depends on the facts being verified, and a self-selected critical field would carry no information within a week. An intake that arrives claiming high is honoured as high until assessed.

## 4. Reassess at Every Touch

Severity describes the situation now, not the situation at intake. Facts arrive: a charging fault becomes a burning smell, a delayed delivery becomes a missed function, a single defect becomes the fifth of its kind. **Upgrades are immediate and never require approval** — an agent who thinks a ticket is more serious than it is marked simply marks it, and being wrong upwards costs almost nothing. Downgrades require a note saying what changed, because the common cause of a downgrade is not new facts but a queue under pressure. Severity is never downgraded to make a target reachable (sla §4).

## 5. Priority, Not Arrival Order

The queue is worked by severity first and age second, not first-in-first-out: an hour-old critical outranks a week-old low, and among equal severities the oldest is served first. Nothing is left indefinitely because something more urgent keeps arriving — the ageing limits in sla §7 exist to stop low-severity tickets starving. Automated classification may suggest a severity, and a human confirms it (intake §7).

## 6. Circumstances That Raise Severity

Some facts about the person, not the fault, change the priority:

- **Medical dependence or a stated health need** for the product — vulnerable §3.
- **A stated time-sensitive event** the customer cannot move — shipping §4.
- **A customer in evident distress**, or language indicating risk to a person — vulnerable §7.
- **Repeat failure**: a third contact on the same issue, or a replacement that failed the same way (tech_support §3).
- **An oversold order** where the customer already made plans around the confirmed delivery date — orders §4.

These raise severity by one level at minimum, and never require the customer to prove the circumstance.

## 7. Severity Sets the Clock, Not the Generosity

What severity changes is how fast we respond and resolve (sla §2, §3) and where the ticket sits in the queue. It does not change what remedy the customer is entitled to: a high-severity return is still handled under returns §1 and a critical safety report under product_safety §4. Remedy comes from the department policy and the authority matrix in authority §2; escalating severity to justify a larger remedy inverts both, and a remedy is never reduced because a ticket was marked low.
