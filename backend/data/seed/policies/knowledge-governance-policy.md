# Knowledge Base & Policy Governance Policy

This policy is company-wide and governs the knowledge base itself: the difference between a policy and a precedent, which source wins when two disagree, how a policy is written and published, and what earns a resolved complaint a permanent place in the corpus.

## 1. Two Kinds of Knowledge

The corpus holds two things that must not be confused. A **policy** says what we are allowed to do — it is authored, approved and normative. A **case** says what we did once — it is a record, and it is evidence of practice, not authority. A case is retrieved because it shows how a policy was applied to a real situation, which is often far more useful than the policy text alone. But **a case never overrides a policy**, and a case that predates a policy change describes a decision we would no longer make. Where a retrieved case and the current policy diverge, the policy governs and the case is a candidate for retirement (§8).

## 2. Precedence When Sources Conflict

1. **Statutory and regulatory law** — legal §8.
2. **The product safety policy**, where a hazard or injury is present.
3. **The legal escalation policy**, where a claim is present.
4. **The owning department's policy** — the specific beats the general.
5. **A company-wide policy**, where no department policy addresses the point.
6. **Case precedent**, as evidence of practice only.

An agent who cannot resolve a conflict with this order refers it under escalation §1, and the conflict itself is a defect in the corpus: it is recorded and fixed under §5 rather than re-adjudicated on every ticket.

## 3. Lifecycle

A policy is **draft** while being written, **in review** while being checked, **published** once approved, **superseded** when a newer version replaces it, and **archived** when it no longer applies to anything current. **Only published policies are retrievable** — a draft must never be citable to a customer or to an agent, because a half-written rule is worse than a missing one. Superseding requires an explicit pointer from the new version to the one it replaced, so a decision made last year can still be read against the policy that was in force at the time. Nothing is deleted; policies are superseded or archived.

## 4. Who Writes, Reviews and Publishes

The owning department drafts and owns its policy's content. Legal reviews anything touching liability, statutory rights, safety language or data protection before it may be published. An admin publishes, and publication is the act that makes it retrievable. A policy needs a named owner: an unowned policy is not reviewed, drifts out of date, and keeps being cited anyway. Company-wide policies are owned by the function that lives with the consequences — the queue's own leadership for intake, severity, SLA and escalation.

## 5. Review Cadence and Triggers

Every policy is reviewed at least annually. Review is also triggered, within 10 business days, by:

- **A systemic incident** whose post-incident review found a policy gap (incident §7).
- **A change in applicable law or regulation** — including a revision to the E-Commerce Rules, the Legal Metrology framework, or the DPDP Act's rules. Anything in this corpus citing a statutory figure (grievance §1–§2, sales §1, privacy §4, §8, payments §5, §7–§8, product_safety §6) is re-verified against the current legal text at this trigger, not amended from memory — **these values are external to us, and this corpus follows them, not the reverse.**
- **A root-caused quality or manufacturing finding** that changes the remedy or the diagnosis (qa §7, manufacturing §7).
- **A referral pattern** — the same question escalated repeatedly means the corpus does not answer it (escalation §8).
- **A conflict found under §2.**

The referral-pattern trigger is the most valuable and the most often skipped: agents hitting the same gap are the cheapest signal the company gets, and it only works if raising it changes something.

## 6. Versions and Effective Dates

Every policy carries a version and an effective date. **A change to a threshold, an authority limit or an obligation is a new version, not an edit** — the old text is retained as superseded so that a decision can be judged against the rule that existed when it was made. Typographical and clarity fixes that change no meaning may be edited in place. Where a version changes what a customer is entitled to, it applies from its effective date forward and is not applied retrospectively to reduce something a customer was already owed.

## 7. Admitting a Resolved Complaint to the Case Corpus

A resolved ticket becomes a retrievable case only if all of the following hold:

- **The outcome was policy-compliant** — a generous mistake is still a mistake, and admitting it teaches the mistake to every future retrieval.
- **It cites the policy that authorised it**, so a future reader can check the reasoning rather than copying the outcome.
- **It has been redacted** per privacy §6.
- **No legal claim is live or reasonably foreseeable** on it (legal §1).
- **It adds something** — a new situation, a boundary case, a clarifying application. The twentieth routine duplicate-charge refund teaches nothing the first one did not.
- **A human approved the admission.** Nothing enters the corpus automatically.

## 8. Retiring a Case

A case that contradicts current policy, that turns out to have been wrongly decided, or whose redaction is found to be incomplete is retired from the corpus. **Cases are retired, not rewritten** — editing the record of what we did to match what we now wish we had done destroys the only thing a case is good for. Where the case is instructive precisely because the outcome was wrong, it may be retained with the correction attached, but only deliberately and never by default. Removing a case removes it from retrieval as well as from the table; an index that still serves a retired case is the same defect it was before.
